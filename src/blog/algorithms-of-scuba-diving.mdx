I fell in love with SCUBA diving on my first dive in the Red Sea, at 16 years old. The colorful corals and fish made the underwater world feel like an alien planet, and a close encounter with a moray eel gave me enough of a thrill to cement that dive as one of my core memories. These days, divers enter that world with a device on their wrist to keep them safe: a watch-sized computer that models how the depths affect the body. It tells them how deep they can go, how long they can stay, and how to return to the surface safely. Given my computer science background, it was inevitable that I'd eventually take a deep dive, pun intended, into how these computers work, specifically, the algorithms keeping divers alive.

## The Decompression Problem

Before we get into those algorithms, we need a rough grasp of decompression theory. Divers breathe gas to stay alive, usually air, the same as we breathe on the surface, but with one important caveat. Physics and anatomy force divers to breathe that air at higher pressure than we do on land, and the deeper they go, the higher that pressure climbs.

That matters because, on the surface, the nitrogen in air is inert: we inhale it, but its partial pressure is too low for it to cross into the bloodstream, so we simply breathe it back out. Underwater, at higher pressure, Henry's Law changes the equation. The nitrogen no longer stays in the lungs, it diffuses into the blood and spreads into the body's tissues. On its own, this still isn't a problem. Nitrogen remains inert in the tissues; it just sits there. We'll conveniently ignore nitrogen narcosis.

The trouble starts on the way up. Think of a carbonated drink: it's bottled under high pressure so that large amounts of CO₂ dissolve into the liquid. Pop the cap, the pressure drops, the equilibrium breaks, and the gas escapes as bubbles. The same thing happens in a diver's body during ascent, dissolved nitrogen comes out of solution as bubbles, and depending on where they form, they can cause all kinds of harm. Ascending in a controlled way that keeps that risk to a minimum is called decompression. A dive computer tracks things like depth and time precisely so it can advise the diver on the safest way back to the surface.

> A decompression algorithm answers one question:
> **Given everything I have breathed so far, how shallow can I safely be right now and how long must I wait before I can go shallower?**

## History Of Decompression Algorithms

Before we do a deep dive into Bühlmann ZH-L16 with Gradient Factors, it's important to understand the history of decompression algorithms, which is really the history of decompression models. At its heart, a decompression model is just a set of mathematical equations that help solvers answer the question described in the previous chapter. None of this began with computers, though. For most of diving's history, these models lived on paper. Researchers ran the math ahead of time and printed the results as dive tables: grids that told you, for a given depth and bottom time, how long to pause at each stop on the way up. A diver planned the whole dive on the surface, then carried a depth gauge and a watch below and followed that plan by hand. The dive computer's real trick wasn't inventing new science, it was taking these same models and running them live, continuously, for the actual dive you were doing rather than the one you had planned.

#### 1908: Haldane

While the history of decompression research goes all the way back to the 17th century, John Scott Haldane was the first to build a model that tried to explain and predict how decompression affects the human body. Commissioned by the Royal Navy and working alongside Arthur Boycott and Guybon Damant, he ran a famous series of experiments on goats to figure out what actually harmed a body on ascent. His two big insights still underpin everything that came after. First, the body doesn't absorb gas all at once: different tissues load and unload nitrogen at different speeds, so he modelled the body as a handful of independent "compartments," each with its own half-time. Second, a diver can tolerate a certain amount of over-pressure before bubbles form. He put that limit at a 2:1 ratio, meaning you could safely halve the pressure in one step, which is how the idea of staged decompression stops was born.

#### 1965: Workman

Working at the US Navy Experimental Diving Unit, Robert Workman took Haldane's framework and made it more precise. Haldane's single 2:1 ratio was a blunt instrument, so Workman's key contribution was realising that each tissue compartment has its own tolerance, and that this tolerance changes with depth. He expressed this as **M-values**: the maximum inert gas pressure a given compartment can hold at a given depth before you risk trouble. This reframed the whole problem from "don't exceed one magic ratio" to "stay under a specific, depth-dependent limit for each compartment," which is the language decompression models still speak today.

#### 1966: Hills

Brian Hills, an Australian researcher working with D.H. LeMessurier, approached the problem from a completely different angle. Where everyone else assumed the goal was to keep gas fully dissolved and avoid bubbles entirely, Hills argued that some tiny amount of bubbling is inevitable and that the real question is how to manage it. His **thermodynamic model** was grounded in phase equilibrium and, interestingly, in the hard-won empirical practices of Torres Strait pearl divers, who ascended in a way that looked nothing like the Navy tables, with deeper stops and less total decompression, yet worked. Hills' work never became mainstream on its own, but it planted the seed for the bubble models that appeared decades later.

#### 1971: DCIEM

Developed at Canada's Defence and Civil Institute of Environmental Medicine, the Kidd-Stubbs model (named for D.J. Kidd and R.A. Stubbs, whose programme began back in 1962) broke with the standard picture in one important way. Instead of treating tissue compartments as parallel and independent, it arranged them **in series**: gas had to pass through one compartment to reach the next, like water filling a chain of connected buckets. The result was a more conservative model that DCIEM refined through an unusually thorough programme of laboratory and real-world human testing, producing tables trusted for decades in Canadian and commercial diving.

#### 1983: Thalmann EL

Edward Thalmann, working for the US Navy, built the Exponential-Linear model to fix a specific weakness in the old approach. He originally designed it around 1980 for divers using the Mk15 rebreather, and published the EL model in 1983. Classic Haldanian models assume gas leaves a tissue just as fast as it enters, but Thalmann's insight was that once bubbles start forming, off-gassing slows down. His model treats on-gassing as exponential but off-gassing as linear, making it asymmetric. This produced safer schedules for the long, deep dives the Navy cared about, and it was later refined into the VVAL-18 parameter set that underpins the modern US Navy tables.

#### 1983: Bühlmann ZH-L12

This is where decompression theory reached the general public. Albert A. Bühlmann, a physician at the University Hospital in Zurich, spent decades refining the Haldane-Workman approach, and crucially, he published it openly rather than locking it inside a navy. The name encodes its origin: **ZH** for Zurich, **L** for limits, **12** for the initial set of coefficients. Bühlmann also tuned his model to work at altitude, not just at sea level, which made it practical for lakes and mountains. Almost every recreational dive table and computer in use today traces its lineage back to his work.

#### 1986: Bühlmann ZH-L16

A refinement of the 1983 model, ZH-L16 replaced the older coefficient set with a more carefully tuned one spanning 16 tissue compartments, with half-times ranging from a few minutes to over ten hours. Bühlmann published several variants (ZH-L16A, B and C) offering slightly different levels of conservatism. This is the version that became the workhorse of the dive computer industry, and it's the model we'll be implementing later in this article.

#### 1986: VPM

The Varying Permeability Model, developed by physicist David Yount and his colleague Hoffman at the University of Hawaii, was the first bubble model to gain real traction. Building on their 1984 study of how bubbles form in gelatin, they brought that physics into diving: they assumed the body is full of microscopic pre-existing gas nuclei, and that decompression sickness happens when these nuclei grow into problematic bubbles. Rather than limiting dissolved gas, VPM limits the **total volume of bubbles** allowed to form, which naturally pushes for deeper, earlier decompression stops than the classic dissolved-gas models.

#### 1990: RGBM

Bruce Wienke, a physicist at Los Alamos National Laboratory, designed the Reduced Gradient Bubble Model to bridge the two worlds: it keeps the practical dissolved-gas bookkeeping of a Bühlmann-style model but adds bubble physics on top. The "reduced gradient" part refers to how it penalises the kinds of profiles known to raise bubble risk, repetitive dives, deep dives and rapid ascents, by tightening the allowed gradients. Manufacturers like Suunto and Mares adopted variants of it later that decade, making RGBM one of the most widely deployed algorithms in modern computers.

#### 1991: TBDM

The Tissue Bubble Diffusion Model, developed by Michael Gernhardt as part of his PhD at the University of Pennsylvania, zoomed in even further than VPM. Instead of tracking a population of nuclei statistically, it models the growth and shrinkage of an individual bubble as gas diffuses in and out of it over time. It's a more physically detailed picture of what a single bubble actually does, and it fed into later commercial and probabilistic decompression work rather than becoming a consumer model in its own right.

#### 1997: BVM(3)

The biophysical, probabilistic models developed for the US Navy by Wayne Gerth and Richard Vann mark a philosophical shift. Rather than defining hard ceilings and declaring anything below them "safe," these models were fit against thousands of real dives with recorded outcomes, and they output a **probability** of decompression sickness for a given profile. Dive planners can then optimise a schedule toward an acceptable risk level rather than a binary pass/fail line, an approach that fed directly into the 2008 US Navy dive tables.

#### 1998: Gradient Factors

The work of Erik Baker, Gradient Factors aren't a new model at all, they're a conservatism knob bolted onto Bühlmann. A gradient factor is simply the fraction of a compartment's allowed over-pressure (its M-value gradient) that you're willing to use, expressed as a low/high pair like 30/85. The low number adds deeper stops, the high number pads the shallow ones. Because it's simple, tunable and transparent, this approach has become the standard way divers dial in how aggressive or cautious their Bühlmann computer behaves.

#### 2001: VPM-B

Erik Baker also took Yount's Varying Permeability Model and added a practical correction. The **B** stands for Boyle's law: as a diver ascends, any bubble that has already formed expands as the surrounding pressure drops, and VPM-B accounts for that expansion when calculating the stops. This tweak made the model more conservative in the shallows and turned VPM into a popular choice among technical divers running desktop decompression planners.
