## The Decompression Problem

Before we get into those algorithms, we need a rough grasp of decompression theory. Divers breathe gas to stay alive, usually air, the same as we breathe on the surface[^1]. Physics and anatomy force them to breathe it at higher pressure than we do on land, and the deeper they go, the higher that pressure climbs.

Pressure in this article is measured in bar[^2]. One bar is roughly the weight of the atmosphere pressing down on you at sea level. Descend 10 metres into the ocean and the water above you adds another bar, so a diver at 30 metres is breathing gas at about 4 bar, four times the pressure at the surface.

Right now, sitting on the surface, your body is already full of nitrogen. Air is about 79% nitrogen, and [Henry's Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry%27s_law)[^3] says the amount of a gas that dissolves into a liquid is proportional to the partial pressure[^4] of that gas above it. At 1 bar of air, your blood and tissues sit at equilibrium with roughly 0.75 bar of dissolved nitrogen and have done since you were born. The nitrogen is inert. It dissolves, it sits there, and nothing happens. We'll conveniently ignore nitrogen narcosis[^5].

Descending changes the pressure, and therefore the equilibrium. A diver at 30 metres is inhaling nitrogen at about 3.1 bar instead of 0.75, so nitrogen starts diffusing out of the lungs, into the blood, and into the tissues until they catch up with the new number. Stay long enough and they get most of the way there. This is called on-gassing, and on its own it still isn't a problem.

The trouble starts on the way back up. Think of a carbonated drink. It's bottled under high pressure so that large amounts of CO₂ dissolve into the liquid. Pop the cap, the pressure drops, the equilibrium breaks, and the gas escapes as bubbles. The same thing happens in a diver's body during ascent. Dissolved nitrogen comes out of solution, and depending on where the bubbles form, in what quantity and volume, they can cause all kinds of harm. Some level of bubbling is normal and the body tolerates it fine. When bubbles start causing harm we call that decompression sickness[^6].

Now shake that bottle and open it slowly. Crack the cap, let some pressure out, wait, crack it further. The drink stays in the bottle. That's the whole idea behind a controlled ascent, and it's what decompression algorithms are built to schedule. A dive computer tracks depth and time precisely so it can tell the diver how far up they can go right now.

> **A decompression algorithm answers one question:**
>
> Given everything I have breathed so far, how shallow can I safely be right now and how long must I wait before I can go shallower?

## Simplified Algorithm 

In this article we'll do a deep dive into arguably the most popular decompression algorithm, the Bühlmann ZH-L16[^7] with Gradient Factors. It's commonly used in dive computers and for building dive tables meant for recreational[^8] and technical[^9] SCUBA divers, while commercial[^10] and military divers normally use different algorithms[^11][^12] custom designed for their needs. Besides ZH-L16, recreational and technical dive computers also use VPM-B[^13] or RGBM[^14] models, but the focus of this article will be ZH-L16 as it seems to be most widely used.

We'll start with a simplified version of the algorithm to master the basic concepts. For starters we'll focus on building an algorithm that can model a single dive to arbitrary depth, the dive will start at sea level and will be an ocean dive, meaning the diver will be diving in saltwater. The diver will also be breathing a single gas mixture of air. Parameters like repetitive dives, altitude of the dive, multiple breathing mixtures, water type all affect the physics of decompression and make the decompression model more complex. We'll also skip gradient factors[^15], for now, and add them later.

### Modeling Human Body

Every decompression algorithm starts from the same unsolved problem: nobody can measure how much nitrogen is dissolved in a diver's blood and tissues in real time. There is no sensor for it. The best anyone can do is model it and check the model against divers who did or didn't get hurt.

In 1908, the physiologist John Scott Haldane[^16] published the first attempt at that model, alongside Arthur Boycott and Guybon Damant[^17]. They ran goats through pressure chambers, compressed them, decompressed them at various rates, and recorded which animals came out with symptoms. Their headline finding was that a goat saturated at some pressure could be brought up to half that pressure without getting hurt, and that the ratio held regardless of the starting depth. A 2:1 reduction was survivable. From that they derived the first staged decompression tables, the ancestor of everything since, ZH-L16 included.

Haldane's real contribution was giving up on anatomy. Rather than model actual organs, he treated the body as a set of theoretical compartments, each a sponge that soaks up and releases gas at its own fixed rate. He used five of them, with half-times of 5, 10, 20, 40 and 75 minutes. Bühlmann ZH-L16[^7] uses sixteen, numbered fastest to slowest. Compartment 1 has a half-time of 4 minutes and stands in for well-perfused, blood-rich tissue such as blood itself or the brain. Compartment 16 has a half-time of 635 minutes, over 10 hours, and stands in for poorly perfused tissue like fat, ligaments, or cartilage. None of them correspond to a specific organ. They're a mathematical convenience covering the range of speeds real tissue might load and unload nitrogen at, and the number 16 in ZH-L16 refers to them.

A half-time is the time a compartment takes to close half the gap between its current nitrogen tension and where it's heading. Drop a diver to a new depth and every compartment starts chasing the new, higher inspired nitrogen pressure, but none of them jump straight to it. After one half-time, each has closed 50% of the gap. After two, 75%. After three, 87.5%, the same halving pattern as radioactive decay. A compartment never fully saturates in the mathematics, it keeps closing half the remaining gap forever, but after about six half-times it's close enough to treat as done. Compartment 1 is effectively saturated within 24 minutes. Compartment 16 needs over 60 hours.

Each compartment updates independently using the same exponential formula, known as Haldane's equation:

$$
P(t) = P_{alv} + (P_0 - P_{alv}) \times 2^{-t / \text{half-time}}
$$

$P(t)$ is the compartment's nitrogen tension after time $t$, $P_0$ is what it started at, and $P_{alv}$ is the alveolar pressure of nitrogen the diver is currently breathing, essentially the partial pressure of nitrogen at the current depth. $\text{half-time}$ is the compartment's defining property. A fast compartment reacts quickly to a change in depth; a slow one barely notices a short dive. Because every compartment runs this formula on its own clock, a single dive profile produces 16 different loading curves, and the algorithm tracks all of them at once.

Let's start turning this into code. A compartment only needs to remember two numbers, its half-time and its current tension, and it only needs to know how to do one thing, update itself given the pressure it's currently breathing and how long it's been breathing it:

```rust
#[derive(Debug, Clone, Copy)]
struct Compartment {
    half_time: f64, // minutes
    tension: f64,   // current N2 tension, in bar
}

impl Compartment {
    fn update(&mut self, p_alv: f64, elapsed_min: f64) {
        let decay = 2f64.powf(-elapsed_min / self.half_time);
        self.tension = p_alv + (self.tension - p_alv) * decay;
    }
}
```

The 16 half-times are Bühlmann's, published as part of ZH-L16[^7], ordered fastest to slowest, in minutes:

```rust
const HALF_TIMES: [f64; 16] = [
    4.0, 8.0, 12.5, 18.5, 27.0, 38.3, 54.3, 77.0, 109.0, 146.0, 187.0, 239.0, 305.0, 390.0,
    498.0, 635.0,
];
```

A diver who's been breathing surface air for long enough starts every dive with all 16 compartments already at equilibrium, so we can build the whole tissue model from that single starting tension:

```rust
fn new_tissue_model(surface_n2: f64) -> [Compartment; 16] {
    HALF_TIMES.map(|half_time| Compartment {
        half_time,
        tension: surface_n2,
    })
}
```

### M Values

Tracking nitrogen loading is only half the problem. A tissue tension on its own tells a diver nothing, it needs something to be measured against. That something is a ceiling value for every compartment: the maximum nitrogen tension it can hold at a given ambient pressure before the risk of bubble formation becomes unacceptable.

These ceilings are called M-values, and they came out of the US Navy rather than Switzerland. Robert Workman introduced them in 1965[^18], replacing Haldane's fixed 2:1 ratio with the observation that each compartment tolerates a different absolute overpressure, and that the tolerance itself changes with depth. Workman expressed them as a surfacing value plus a slope per metre of depth. Bühlmann kept the idea and rewrote the line in a form anchored on ambient pressure instead of depth, which is what made it portable to altitude diving[^19]:

$$
M = a + \frac{P_{amb}}{b}
$$

$P_{amb}$ is the ambient pressure at the diver's current depth, and $M$ is the maximum nitrogen tension that compartment is allowed to hold at that pressure. Each of the 16 compartments has its own $a$ and $b$. Fast compartments have M-value lines that tolerate a large overpressure, since any excess nitrogen they're holding gets released again quickly. Slow compartments tolerate far less, they don't off-gas fast enough to make a large overpressure safe.

The M-value turns a tissue tension into an actionable number: the ceiling depth, the shallowest depth at which none of the 16 compartments would exceed their M-value. As long as a diver stays at or below their current ceiling, ascending further is safe. As soon as one compartment's tension would cross its M-value line, that compartment becomes the controlling compartment, and the diver has to stop ascending until it off-gasses enough for the ceiling to lift.

Back in the code, each `Compartment` picks up its `a` and `b` coefficients, plus a way to evaluate its own M-value at a given ambient pressure and, by rearranging that same line, the ambient pressure at which its current tension would sit exactly on the M-value:

```rust
#[derive(Debug, Clone, Copy)]
struct Compartment {
    half_time: f64, // minutes
    tension: f64,   // current N2 tension, in bar
    a: f64,         // M-value coefficient
    b: f64,         // M-value coefficient
}

impl Compartment {
    fn update(&mut self, p_alv: f64, elapsed_min: f64) {
        let decay = 2f64.powf(-elapsed_min / self.half_time);
        self.tension = p_alv + (self.tension - p_alv) * decay;
    }

    fn m_value(&self, ambient_pressure: f64) -> f64 {
        self.a + ambient_pressure / self.b
    }

    fn ceiling_pressure(&self) -> f64 {
        (self.tension - self.a) * self.b
    }
}
```

`ceiling_pressure` is the M-value formula solved for `ambient_pressure` instead of `M`. It answers "at what ambient pressure would this compartment's current tension be exactly at its M-value?" That's the compartment's own ceiling. Bühlmann published `a` and `b` for all 16 compartments alongside the half-times:


#### ZH-L16A vs. ZH-L16B vs. ZH-L16C

Bühlmann's coefficients don't come as a single fixed table. There are three published sets, A, B, and C, and which one a given piece of software implements changes how conservative the resulting model is.

ZH-L16A[^7] is the original, purely mathematical dataset. Bühlmann didn't fit these by hand, he derived $a$ and $b$ directly from each compartment's half-time with a closed-form regression:

$$
a = 2 \times \text{half-time}^{-1/3} \qquad b = 1.005 - \text{half-time}^{-1/2}
$$

You can check this against the coefficient table above. It's an exact match, that table is ZH-L16A. When Bühlmann tested A against real dive data, he found it wasn't conservative enough in the middle compartments[^19], the ones that end up controlling most real-world dive profiles, so he published two manually adjusted variants on top of it.

ZH-L16B tightens the $a$ coefficient for compartments 6, 7, 8, and 13, leaving the rest of the table equal to A[^7]. It's the set Bühlmann intended for producing printed dive tables, where the table's own rounding to whole minutes and metres would otherwise erode the small margin those compartments needed.

ZH-L16C tightens $a$ further, across compartments 5 through 15, and is the set Bühlmann intended for direct use in dive computers, since a computer tracks depth and time exactly and doesn't get the incidental conservatism that comes from reading a rounded table[^7]. It's the most conservative of the three.

Copy the B or C tables out of a published source rather than deriving them and you inherit a wrinkle. Both variants adjust only $a$, so every $b$ should still be the closed-form value, and fifteen of the sixteen are. Compartment 4 is published as 0.7825 where the formula gives 0.7725. One digit, in one compartment, and it has been carried faithfully into every implementation I checked, Subsurface[^32] included. Correcting it now would mean disagreeing with the schedules every dive computer on the market produces, so nobody does.

In practice, dive computer manufacturers rarely implement any of the three untouched. Most apply their own additional conservatism on top, so there's no single variant every computer on the market agrees on. For the rest of this article, and the code, we'll keep using the closed-form A coefficients already in `COEFFICIENTS`. They're the ones we can derive and verify ourselves, and Gradient Factors, covered later in this article, are a more transparent and more widely supported way to dial in the kind of extra conservatism B and C were hand-tuned for. Bear in mind while reading the numbers that come out of this model that raw ZH-L16A is the least conservative thing you can build out of Bühlmann's work.

### Putting It All Together

With Haldane's equation to track nitrogen loading and M-values to define a safe ceiling, the algorithm itself is mostly a loop. At every step of a dive, sampled every few seconds on a real dive computer, it does the same four things:

1. Read the diver's current depth and use it to compute the ambient pressure, and from that, the partial pressure of nitrogen currently being breathed.
2. Update all 16 compartments using Haldane's equation, based on how long the diver has been at that depth.
3. Compute each compartment's M-value at the current ambient pressure and compare it to that compartment's tension.
4. Find the controlling compartment, whichever one has the smallest margin to its M-value, and use its M-value line to compute the current ceiling depth.

If the ceiling is at or above the surface, the diver is within their no-decompression limit[^20] and can ascend directly. If the ceiling is below the surface, the diver has to stop, wait at that depth while the controlling compartment off-gasses, and let the algorithm recompute the ceiling. In practice this produces the staged decompression stops divers are trained to perform: ascend to the ceiling, rounded up to the next 3 metre increment, hold until the ceiling rises, ascend again, repeat until reaching the surface.

The safety stop is a different animal, and gets confused with all of this constantly. It's the three minutes at five metres that recreational divers make at the end of almost every dive. No model asks for it. The computer has already said the ceiling is at the surface and the diver is free to go up. It's a habit built on the knowledge that the model is a curve fit over a population, that any individual diver might be having a worse day than the average one it was fitted to, and that three minutes is cheap. The stops this chapter computes are mandatory in a way a safety stop is not.

Wrapping the 16 compartments in a `Diver` gives us a home for that loop. `at_surface` builds the tissue model the same way `new_tissue_model` did earlier, but now also seeds each compartment's `a` and `b`:

```rust
const SURFACE_PRESSURE: f64 = 1.0; // bar
const WATER_VAPOR_PRESSURE: f64 = 0.0627; // bar, in the lungs at body temperature
const FRACTION_N2: f64 = 0.79; // breathing air

struct Diver {
    compartments: [Compartment; 16],
}

impl Diver {
    fn at_surface() -> Self {
        let surface_n2 = FRACTION_N2 * (SURFACE_PRESSURE - WATER_VAPOR_PRESSURE);
        Diver {
            compartments: std::array::from_fn(|i| Compartment {
                half_time: HALF_TIMES[i],
                tension: surface_n2,
                a: COEFFICIENTS[i].0,
                b: COEFFICIENTS[i].1,
            }),
        }
    }
}
```

`WATER_VAPOR_PRESSURE` shows up because the air a diver inhales is humidified in the lungs before nitrogen's partial pressure is calculated. That fixed 0.0627 bar of water vapour[^7] displaces breathing gas and never carries nitrogen, so it has to be subtracted out first. `FRACTION_N2` is 0.79 rather than air's true 78.08% nitrogen, because diving convention lumps argon and the other trace gases in with nitrogen and treats the result as one inert gas.

With the diver built, `step` and `ceiling_depth` are the four-item loop from above, written out directly:

```rust
impl Diver {
    fn step(&mut self, depth_m: f64, elapsed_min: f64) {
        let ambient = SURFACE_PRESSURE + depth_m / 10.0;
        let p_alv = FRACTION_N2 * (ambient - WATER_VAPOR_PRESSURE);
        for c in &mut self.compartments {
            c.update(p_alv, elapsed_min);
        }
    }

    fn ceiling_depth(&self) -> f64 {
        let ceiling_pressure = self
            .compartments
            .iter()
            .map(Compartment::ceiling_pressure)
            .fold(SURFACE_PRESSURE, f64::max);
        ((ceiling_pressure - SURFACE_PRESSURE) * 10.0).max(0.0)
    }
}
```

`step` folds the ambient-pressure and inspired-nitrogen math into one call and updates every compartment with it. `ceiling_depth` is the controlling-compartment search: take the highest ceiling pressure across all 16 compartments, since that's the compartment closest to its M-value, and convert it to a depth, clamped at zero because a ceiling can never be shallower than the surface. Simulating a 30 metre dive, sampled once a minute, is now a few lines:

```rust
let mut diver = Diver::at_surface();

for minute in 1..=45 {
    diver.step(30.0, 1.0);
    let ceiling = diver.ceiling_depth();
    if ceiling > 0.0 {
        println!("{minute} min: ceiling now at {ceiling:.1}m, ascent must stop there");
    }
}
```

Run that and the diver is clear to surface for the first 16 minutes. At 17 minutes a ceiling appears, 14 centimetres of it, and it grows the longer the bottom time stretches: 0.95 m at 20 minutes, 2.9 m at 30, 4.8 m at 45. Four fields per compartment, two small formulas, and a loop.

Sixteen minutes is a shorter no-decompression limit than a real computer would give you for 30 metres, where the answer is usually around 20. That gap is the price of raw ZH-L16A with no conservatism layered on top. Moving that line is what the B and C tables were for, and what Gradient Factors do later in this article.

### Ascent Rates

There's a cheat buried in `step` that's worth naming before we build anything on top of it. Haldane's equation assumes the inspired pressure is constant for the whole interval. That's true when a diver sits at one depth, and it's false during every descent and ascent, which is when the pressure is changing fastest and when the algorithm's answer matters most.

Sampling often enough hides the problem. A real dive computer calls something like `step` every second or two with the depth it just measured, and a one-second block of a 10 metre-per-minute ascent is close enough to constant that nobody cares. Dive *planning* software has the opposite problem: it wants to project an ascent that hasn't happened yet, in one calculation, over minutes at a time. For that there's an exact solution, published by Schreiner and Kelley in 1971[^21], which extends Haldane's equation with a term for a linearly changing inspired pressure:

$$
P(t) = P_{alv_0} + R\left(t - \frac{1}{k}\right) - \left(P_{alv_0} - P_0 - \frac{R}{k}\right)e^{-kt}
$$

$R$ is the rate the inspired inert gas pressure changes at, in bar per minute, and $k$ is $\ln 2$ divided by the half-time. Set $R$ to zero and the whole thing collapses back into Haldane's equation. It's the same model, integrated properly across a ramp instead of a step, and it's why ascent rate is a real input to a decompression schedule rather than an afterthought. Ascend slower and the fast compartments get more time to dump gas before the ceiling is evaluated. Ascend faster and they don't. Recreational training caps ascents at 18 metres per minute, and 10 metres per minute is the common technical figure.

The constant-pressure form is fine for the bottom and for stops, where the diver isn't moving, so that's what the rest of this article uses. We'll come back for this equation when we build an ascent schedule, because that's where the difference stops being academic. I know it stops being academic because I first wrote that chapter without it, and it gave me a wrong answer I believed for a while.

## Bühlmann ZH-L16
In the previous chapter we modeled a single, no-repeat, sea-level, single-gas dive from start to finish. We deliberately simplified it, ignoring multiple gas mixtures, altitude, salt versus fresh water, and repetitive dives, all of which change the numbers involved but not the shape of the algorithm. In this chapter we'll systematically introduce these concepts and see how the algorithm is altered because of it.

### Salt vs. Fresh Water

Everything so far has quietly assumed one ambient pressure formula, one bar at the surface plus one bar for every 10 metres of depth. That 10 metres per bar comes from the density of the water pressing down on the diver, and it's a seawater number. The depth of water needed to produce one bar is:

$$
h = \frac{100{,}000}{\rho \times 9.80665}
$$

Fresh water, at 1000 kg per cubic metre, needs 10.2 metres to add a bar. Seawater is denser thanks to dissolved salt, around 1020 to 1030 kg per cubic metre depending on where you are, which works out to somewhere between 9.9 and 10.0 metres. The familiar "10 metres is a bar" rule is that seawater figure rounded off, and diving has used it long enough that it may as well be exact. The difference matters only in that the same reading on a depth gauge loads a diver with slightly less nitrogen in a lake than in the ocean, because the water column is doing slightly less work per metre.

The fix is to stop hardcoding that `/ 10.0` from the previous chapter's `step` and turn it into a property of the dive:

```rust
const SALT_WATER_M_PER_BAR: f64 = 10.0;
const FRESH_WATER_M_PER_BAR: f64 = 10.2;

struct Diver {
    compartments: [Compartment; 16],
    meters_per_bar: f64,
}

impl Diver {
    fn at_surface(meters_per_bar: f64) -> Self {
        let surface_n2 = FRACTION_N2 * (SURFACE_PRESSURE - WATER_VAPOR_PRESSURE);
        Diver {
            compartments: std::array::from_fn(|i| Compartment {
                half_time: HALF_TIMES[i],
                tension: surface_n2,
                a: COEFFICIENTS[i].0,
                b: COEFFICIENTS[i].1,
            }),
            meters_per_bar,
        }
    }

    fn step(&mut self, depth_m: f64, elapsed_min: f64) {
        let ambient = SURFACE_PRESSURE + depth_m / self.meters_per_bar;
        let p_alv = FRACTION_N2 * (ambient - WATER_VAPOR_PRESSURE);
        for c in &mut self.compartments {
            c.update(p_alv, elapsed_min);
        }
    }

    fn ceiling_depth(&self) -> f64 {
        let ceiling_pressure = self
            .compartments
            .iter()
            .map(Compartment::ceiling_pressure)
            .fold(SURFACE_PRESSURE, f64::max);
        ((ceiling_pressure - SURFACE_PRESSURE) * self.meters_per_bar).max(0.0)
    }
}
```

`ceiling_depth` needed the same treatment, converting a ceiling pressure back into metres has to use the same water density the dive was in. Run the same 30 metre, 30 minute dive through both constructors and the seawater diver ends up with a 2.94 m ceiling against the fresh water diver's 2.72 m. The lighter water column meant a lower ambient pressure, 3.96 bar instead of 4.01, for the same depth gauge reading.

### Repetitive Dives

Surfacing doesn't mean decompression is over. It means ambient pressure stopped decreasing while the tissues are still catching up to it. A diver who surfaces with that 2.9 metre ceiling from the earlier simulation hasn't finished off-gassing, they've just moved the rest of it above water, where ambient pressure happens to be close to what their compartments still need to reach. If that diver jumped straight into a second dive, every compartment would start it already partially loaded. This is why a second dive can't be planned as if it were the first one: the starting state depends on how loaded the diver was when they surfaced and how much time has passed since.

This one's almost a non-event in code. A printed dive table has to represent "how loaded is this diver right now" as a letter grade, a repetitive group, because a table can't carry 16 continuously updating numbers around with you. Between dives it hands you a second table to convert that letter and a surface interval into a new starting letter for the next dive. It's a clever way to approximate a continuous process using paper, and it's still an approximation.

Our `Diver` doesn't have that problem. It never resets, the 16 compartments hold their actual tension between calls to `step`, so a surface interval is time spent at zero depth:

```rust
impl Diver {
    fn surface_interval(&mut self, minutes: f64) {
        self.step(0.0, minutes);
    }
}
```

Calling `diver.step(0.0, 1.0)` sixty times to simulate an hour on the boat does the same math as diving at 0 metres. The ambient pressure comes out to `SURFACE_PRESSURE`, and every compartment off-gasses toward it at its own half-time, the way it would mid-dive. Chain a second `step` sequence for a repetitive dive straight after and the compartments keep going from wherever the surface interval left them. Table-based systems need dedicated repetitive-dive logic because they discretize state into letters. A real-time model never loses that state to begin with.

### Altitude Diving

Diving at altitude, in a mountain lake, changes the one number we've been treating as a constant since the first version of this algorithm: `SURFACE_PRESSURE`. At sea level that's 1 bar by definition, but climb to a 2000 metre lake and there's less atmosphere overhead pressing down, so the starting pressure is lower. Every ambient pressure calculation in the algorithm, and every M-value, is anchored to that number, so getting it right matters as much as getting the water density right.

Atmospheric pressure as a function of altitude follows the standard barometric formula[^22]:

$$
P_{atm}(h) = 1.01325 \times \left(1 - 2.25577 \times 10^{-5} \times h\right)^{5.25588}
$$

```rust
fn atmospheric_pressure(altitude_m: f64) -> f64 {
    1.01325 * (1.0 - 2.25577e-5 * altitude_m).powf(5.25588)
}

struct Diver {
    compartments: [Compartment; 16],
    meters_per_bar: f64,
    surface_pressure: f64,
}

impl Diver {
    fn at_altitude(altitude_m: f64, meters_per_bar: f64) -> Self {
        let surface_pressure = atmospheric_pressure(altitude_m);
        let surface_n2 = FRACTION_N2 * (surface_pressure - WATER_VAPOR_PRESSURE);
        Diver {
            compartments: std::array::from_fn(|i| Compartment {
                half_time: HALF_TIMES[i],
                tension: surface_n2,
                a: COEFFICIENTS[i].0,
                b: COEFFICIENTS[i].1,
            }),
            meters_per_bar,
            surface_pressure,
        }
    }

    fn step(&mut self, depth_m: f64, elapsed_min: f64) {
        let ambient = self.surface_pressure + depth_m / self.meters_per_bar;
        let p_alv = FRACTION_N2 * (ambient - WATER_VAPOR_PRESSURE);
        for c in &mut self.compartments {
            c.update(p_alv, elapsed_min);
        }
    }

    fn ceiling_depth(&self) -> f64 {
        let ceiling_pressure = self
            .compartments
            .iter()
            .map(Compartment::ceiling_pressure)
            .fold(self.surface_pressure, f64::max);
        ((ceiling_pressure - self.surface_pressure) * self.meters_per_bar).max(0.0)
    }
}
```

`at_surface` becomes a special case of `at_altitude` at 0 metres, where sea level pressure comes out to 1.0132 bar. That's the standard atmosphere, and it's what the earlier chapters were rounding to 1.0. Run the same 30 metre, 30 minute dive at sea level and in a 2000 metre lake and the mountain dive ends with a 3.68 metre ceiling against 2.94 at sea level. A lower starting pressure means every depth along the way represents a proportionally bigger pressure swing on ascent, which is why altitude diving calls for more conservative profiles in practice.

Altitude doesn't only matter for the dive itself. Off-gassing continues well past the moment a diver leaves the water. If they then climb a mountain pass or board a flight, they're lowering the ambient pressure again, exactly like an ascent, just without any water involved. Do that before the compartments have caught up and it's possible to develop decompression sickness on a chairlift or at cruising altitude, with no dive computer around to warn anyone.

The model already has everything it needs to answer this. A compartment's ceiling is a pressure, not a depth, and pressure behaves the same whether it's water or air doing the pressing. All that's missing is comparing that pressure against a destination directly instead of converting it to a depth first:

```rust
impl Diver {
    fn controlling_pressure(&self) -> f64 {
        self.compartments
            .iter()
            .map(Compartment::ceiling_pressure)
            .fold(f64::NEG_INFINITY, f64::max)
    }

    fn ceiling_depth(&self) -> f64 {
        ((self.controlling_pressure() - self.surface_pressure) * self.meters_per_bar).max(0.0)
    }

    fn clear_for_altitude(&self, altitude_m: f64) -> bool {
        atmospheric_pressure(altitude_m) >= self.controlling_pressure()
    }
}
```

`controlling_pressure` pulls the shared max-across-compartments logic out of `ceiling_depth`, which now expresses that same pressure as a depth below the current dive. Note that it folds from negative infinity rather than from `surface_pressure`, because the whole point here is to let the answer come out below the surface. `clear_for_altitude` skips the depth conversion and asks the direct question: is the destination's atmospheric pressure still at or above what the slowest-clearing compartment currently needs?

Simulate the 30 metre, 30 minute dive, surface, and step through the surface interval a minute at a time checking `clear_for_altitude` against a typical airliner cabin, pressurized to roughly 2400 metres or 0.756 bar[^23], and the model says clear to fly after 17 minutes. Real-world guidance from DAN recommends a minimum of 12 hours, and longer for repetitive or decompression dives[^24].

That's a factor of forty, and it's tempting to explain it away as missing conservatism. It isn't. I tried: rerunning the same profile with a hard cap of 30% of each M-value, far more conservative than anything a diver would actually set, still clears the flight after about three hours. No plausible setting of the model produces twelve.

The reason is that the two numbers were never derived the same way. Bühlmann's M-values were fitted to predict symptomatic decompression sickness in divers surfacing at the pressure they started from. Flying-after-diving guidance comes from a completely separate body of evidence: chamber trials at DAN and elsewhere that put real divers through real profiles and then decompressed them to altitude, looking for symptoms[^24]. The 12 hour figure is an empirical result, and it exists precisely because the dissolved-gas model wasn't a reliable predictor of it. It's the sharpest divergence in this article between what the model says and what the diving world does, and a useful reminder of what these 16 compartments actually are: a curve fitted to observations, reliable inside the envelope it was fitted to and speculative outside it.

### Nitrox Diving

Nitrox, or enriched air, replaces some of the nitrogen a diver breathes with extra oxygen. EAN32 means 32% oxygen and 68% nitrogen, instead of air's 21% and 79%. Nothing about the algorithm changes to support it. `FRACTION_N2` was already the one input controlling how much nitrogen ends up in `p_alv`, it stops being a constant:

```rust
const AIR_FRACTION_N2: f64 = 0.79;

impl Diver {
    fn step(&mut self, depth_m: f64, elapsed_min: f64, fraction_n2: f64) {
        let ambient = self.surface_pressure + depth_m / self.meters_per_bar;
        let p_alv = fraction_n2 * (ambient - WATER_VAPOR_PRESSURE);
        for c in &mut self.compartments {
            c.update(p_alv, elapsed_min);
        }
    }
}
```

`surface_interval` now has to forward a gas too, and air is the reasonable default for time spent on a boat: `self.step(0.0, minutes, AIR_FRACTION_N2)`. Running the same 30 metre, 30 minute dive on air and on EAN32 side by side, the air ceiling comes out at 2.94 m and the EAN32 ceiling at 0.40 m. Less nitrogen in the mix means less nitrogen dissolved into every compartment for the same depth and time, which is the entire appeal of nitrox for recreational diving: more bottom time, or more margin, for the same profile.

Nitrox doesn't only touch nitrogen. Every fraction taken away from nitrogen goes to oxygen, and more oxygen brings its own ceiling, one that has nothing to do with any of the 16 compartments. Breathed at a high enough partial pressure, oxygen stresses the central nervous system directly, in a way unrelated to nitrogen loading, and beyond some threshold that stress can trigger a seizure underwater. It arrives without much warning and it's frequently fatal, because a seizing diver usually loses their regulator[^25]. The convention is to cap the partial pressure of oxygen in a working gas at 1.4 bar[^25], and the maximum operating depth, or MOD, is the depth at which a given mix's oxygen fraction reaches that cap:

```rust
const MAX_PO2: f64 = 1.4; // bar, conventional working limit
const MOD_SURFACE_PRESSURE: f64 = 1.0; // MOD is reckoned against a flat 1 bar

impl Diver {
    fn mod_for(&self, fraction_o2: f64) -> f64 {
        let ambient = MAX_PO2 / fraction_o2;
        ((ambient - MOD_SURFACE_PRESSURE) * self.meters_per_bar).max(0.0)
    }
}
```

That second constant is deliberate. Everywhere else in the algorithm we've been careful to use the real surface pressure, 1.01325 bar at sea level, but MOD is conventionally reckoned against a flat 1 bar. It's a small difference, about 13 centimetres of depth, and every agency's published tables bake it in, so a model that "corrects" it just disagrees with the numbers divers are trained on. Hold on to that, because it comes back in the Multigas chapter with more consequences than 13 centimetres suggests.

For air's 21% oxygen the limit comes out at 56.7 metres, deeper than most recreational divers ever go, which is why it rarely comes up on air. For EAN32 it drops to 33.8 metres. The same enrichment that buys extra no-decompression time up top puts a hard ceiling on how deep the gas can be taken at all.

1.4 bar is a safety margin rather than a physiological cliff edge. The US Navy and NOAA built their oxygen exposure limits from experimental data on how long divers could tolerate elevated PO2 before showing CNS symptoms: tremors, tunnel vision, nausea, twitching, the warning signs that can precede a seizure. Risk climbs steeply and unpredictably somewhere above 1.6 bar, worse with exertion, cold, and CO2 retention, all of which are hard to control for and vary from diver to diver[^25]. The US Navy caps closed-circuit rebreather exposure at 1.3 bar. NOAA allows 1.3 bar for up to 180 minutes, or 240 in exceptional cases[^25]. PADI settled on 1.4 bar as the recreational open-circuit convention because it sits below all of that with room to spare. The stretch between 1.4 and 1.6 bar gets called the "yellow light zone" in technical diving[^26]: low probability of toxicity, thin margin for error. 1.6 is reserved for decompression stops, where a diver is stationary and resting rather than working[^25][^26]. `MAX_PO2` in the code above is a convention, not a law of physiology, and different agencies and different phases of the same dive draw the line in different places.

A threshold alone isn't the whole story, because CNS risk depends on how long the diver sits near it as well as how close they get. NOAA publishes a table of maximum single-exposure times at each PO2: 45 minutes at 1.6 bar, 120 at 1.5, 150 at 1.4, 180 at 1.3, 210 at 1.2[^27]. Dive computers turn that table into a running percentage, the CNS clock, by charging each interval as a fraction of the limit at whatever PO2 the diver is breathing:

$$
\text{CNS\%} = \sum \frac{t_i}{\text{limit}(P_{O_2,i})} \times 100
$$

Ten minutes at 1.6 bar spends 22% of the budget; ten minutes at 1.3 spends 6%. Computers alarm as the total approaches 100%, carry it between dives, and decay it on the surface with a half-time of around 90 minutes, which makes it the same kind of running state as the 16 compartments, tracked against a completely different failure mode.

CNS toxicity is the acute one. There's a second, slower kind. Prolonged exposure to elevated oxygen, even well below the seizure threshold, irritates the lungs, and dive planning software tracks that separately as oxygen toxicity units, or OTU, using a formula built on the same "how far above a baseline pressure" idea as an M-value[^27]:

$$
\text{OTU} = t \times \left(\frac{P_{O_2} - 0.5}{0.5}\right)^{0.83}
$$

Below 0.5 bar of oxygen, pulmonary toxicity is negligible and no OTU accumulate. Above it, this needs the same two ingredients `step` already computes every call, the current ambient pressure and the oxygen fraction, so it slots in as one more line rather than a separate system:

```rust
struct Diver {
    compartments: [Compartment; 16],
    meters_per_bar: f64,
    surface_pressure: f64,
    otu: f64,
}

impl Diver {
    fn step(&mut self, depth_m: f64, elapsed_min: f64, fraction_n2: f64) {
        let ambient = self.surface_pressure + depth_m / self.meters_per_bar;
        let p_alv = fraction_n2 * (ambient - WATER_VAPOR_PRESSURE);
        for c in &mut self.compartments {
            c.update(p_alv, elapsed_min);
        }

        let po2 = (1.0 - fraction_n2) * ambient;
        if po2 > 0.5 {
            self.otu += elapsed_min * ((po2 - 0.5) / 0.5).powf(0.83);
        }
    }
}
```

Since this simplified model still only tracks nitrogen and oxygen, the oxygen fraction falls straight out of the nitrogen fraction already being passed in, `1.0 - fraction_n2`, no new parameter needed yet. An hour at 30 metres on EAN32 puts the diver at a PO2 of 1.28 bar and accumulates 87 OTU. The REPEX limits, the ones most planning software follows, allow about 850 OTU on a single day of diving, dropping to roughly 700 per day over two days and 300 or so per day across a ten-day trip[^28]. So `otu` is a running total a real dive computer has to carry across an entire holiday, not just a single dive, the same way the compartments themselves do.

### Trimix Diving

Trimix adds a second inert gas, helium, mainly to cut narcosis and gas density at depth. It forces a real structural change, because nitrogen is no longer the only gas being tracked. Each compartment needs its own helium tension, its own helium half-time, and its own helium $a$ and $b$. Bühlmann published a full second table for it, with half-times about 2.65 times shorter than the matching nitrogen compartment, reflecting how much faster helium diffuses[^7][^19]:

```rust
const HE_HALF_TIMES: [f64; 16] = [
    1.88, 3.02, 4.72, 6.99, 10.21, 14.48, 20.53, 29.11, 41.20, 55.19, 70.69, 90.34, 115.29,
    147.42, 188.24, 240.03,
];

const HE_COEFFICIENTS: [(f64, f64); 16] = [
    (1.6189, 0.4770),
    (1.3830, 0.5747),
    (1.1919, 0.6527),
    (1.0458, 0.7223),
    (0.9220, 0.7582),
    (0.8205, 0.7957),
    (0.7305, 0.8279),
    (0.6502, 0.8553),
    (0.5950, 0.8757),
    (0.5545, 0.8903),
    (0.5333, 0.8997),
    (0.5189, 0.9073),
    (0.5181, 0.9122),
    (0.5176, 0.9171),
    (0.5172, 0.9217),
    (0.5119, 0.9267),
];
```

Unlike nitrogen's A-set, this table can't be regenerated from the closed-form regression. Feed the helium half-times into $a = 2 \times h^{-1/3}$ and the results match the published $a$ column to four decimal places for compartments 1 through 8, then drift apart, ending 0.19 too low by compartment 16. The slow helium compartments were adjusted by hand. The $b$ column never matches the formula at all, at any compartment, and is a separately fitted set. Two of those numbers, the 1.88 minute first half-time and its 1.6189 coefficient, also pair with the B and C nitrogen tables, whose fastest compartment is 5 minutes rather than A's 4.

`Compartment` doubles up, one half-time, tension, and $a$/$b$ pair per gas. A compartment doesn't get two separate ceilings, it gets one combined M-value, blended from both gases in proportion to how much of each it's currently holding[^19]:

```rust
#[derive(Debug, Clone, Copy)]
struct Compartment {
    n2_half_time: f64,
    n2_tension: f64,
    n2_a: f64,
    n2_b: f64,
    he_half_time: f64,
    he_tension: f64,
    he_a: f64,
    he_b: f64,
}

impl Compartment {
    fn update(&mut self, p_n2: f64, p_he: f64, elapsed_min: f64) {
        let n2_decay = 2f64.powf(-elapsed_min / self.n2_half_time);
        self.n2_tension = p_n2 + (self.n2_tension - p_n2) * n2_decay;

        let he_decay = 2f64.powf(-elapsed_min / self.he_half_time);
        self.he_tension = p_he + (self.he_tension - p_he) * he_decay;
    }

    fn tension(&self) -> f64 {
        self.n2_tension + self.he_tension
    }

    fn a(&self) -> f64 {
        (self.n2_a * self.n2_tension + self.he_a * self.he_tension) / self.tension()
    }

    fn b(&self) -> f64 {
        (self.n2_b * self.n2_tension + self.he_b * self.he_tension) / self.tension()
    }

    fn m_value(&self, ambient_pressure: f64) -> f64 {
        self.a() + ambient_pressure / self.b()
    }

    fn ceiling_pressure(&self) -> f64 {
        (self.tension() - self.a()) * self.b()
    }
}
```

Splitting `a` and `b` out as methods keeps the rest of the code working unchanged: `m_value` and `ceiling_pressure` read the same as they did in the single-gas version, they just ask for a blended coefficient instead of a stored one. A compartment loaded mostly with nitrogen behaves almost like the single-gas model. One loaded mostly with helium leans on helium's numbers instead. `step` now takes a fraction for each inert gas and feeds both into every compartment's `update`.

Helium loads faster than nitrogen, and the model charges you for that. Run 45 metres for 20 minutes three ways and the raw ceilings come out at 7.3 m on air, 8.0 m on 21/35 trimix, and 9.5 m on 18/47. Both trimixes owe *more* decompression than air, despite the 18/47 carrying less than half air's nitrogen, because helium piles into the fast compartments quicker than nitrogen ever could and those helium $b$ coefficients are unforgiving. Bühlmann's helium numbers also never got the same validation against real dive data that the nitrogen table did[^19], and technical divers generally find helium gentler in practice than a straight ZH-L16 schedule implies. It's a good illustration of what the model is: a curve fitted to observations, doing something plausible but unverified in a region where the observations ran out.

### Multigas Diving

The last piece isn't a new formula. `step` already accepts whatever gas the diver happens to be breathing at each moment, so switching gas mid-dive is calling it again with different fractions. Technical divers routinely carry more than one cylinder: a bottom mix chosen for depth, a travel mix for the shallower part of the ascent, and a rich decompression gas, sometimes pure oxygen, for the final stops, where extra oxygen accelerates off-gassing without adding any inert gas to track.

```rust
let mut diver = Diver::at_surface(SALT_WATER_M_PER_BAR);

// bottom: 21 minutes on 21/35 trimix at 45m
for _ in 0..21 {
    diver.step(45.0, 1.0, 0.44, 0.35);
}

// travel gas: EAN50 at 21m
for _ in 0..3 {
    diver.step(21.0, 1.0, 0.50, 0.0);
}

// deco gas: pure oxygen at 6m
for _ in 0..8 {
    diver.step(6.0, 1.0, 0.0, 0.0);
}
```

Nothing in `Diver` needed to change for this to work. Each `step` call only cares about the gas it's given and the compartments it's updating, it has no memory of which cylinder any of that gas came from. Table-based systems need a distinct table, or a distinct set of rules, for every gas-switching scenario they support. Building the algorithm around ambient pressure and inspired gas fractions from the first version gets multigas diving for free.

That example hardcoded the switch depths, 45 m, 21 m, and 6 m, chosen by hand to match the gases on offer. A real diver plans a dive before getting in the water, and dive planning software doesn't hardcode anything, it searches. Given a set of cylinders the diver intends to carry, it works out, for every depth along the ascent, which of those gases is both breathable, its oxygen partial pressure under the limit, and the richest in oxygen it can get away with, since a leaner inert gas fraction always off-gasses faster and shortens the remaining stops.

The limit it searches against is the 1.6 bar decompression figure from the previous chapter, not the 1.4 bar working figure. That distinction is the whole reason those switch depths land where they do:

```rust
const MAX_PO2_DECO: f64 = 1.6; // bar, resting at a stop

#[derive(Debug, Clone, Copy)]
struct Gas {
    fraction_n2: f64,
    fraction_he: f64,
}

impl Gas {
    fn fraction_o2(&self) -> f64 {
        1.0 - self.fraction_n2 - self.fraction_he
    }
}

fn best_gas_for_depth(gases: &[Gas], depth_m: f64, surface_pressure: f64, meters_per_bar: f64) -> Gas {
    let ambient = surface_pressure + depth_m / meters_per_bar;
    *gases
        .iter()
        .filter(|g| g.fraction_o2() * ambient <= MAX_PO2_DECO)
        .max_by(|a, b| a.fraction_o2().partial_cmp(&b.fraction_o2()).unwrap())
        .expect("no gas in the set is breathable at this depth")
}
```

It takes `surface_pressure` as its own parameter rather than reaching for a constant, since the search has to work for a mountain lake as well as for the ocean. Hand it the same three cylinders from before and let it choose at every stop instead of choosing by hand:

```rust
let deco_gases = [
    Gas { fraction_n2: 0.79, fraction_he: 0.0 }, // air
    Gas { fraction_n2: 0.50, fraction_he: 0.0 }, // EAN50
    Gas { fraction_n2: 0.0, fraction_he: 0.0 },  // oxygen
];

let surface_pressure = MOD_SURFACE_PRESSURE; // the same flat 1 bar as mod_for
let mut depth = 45.0;
while depth > 0.0 {
    let gas = best_gas_for_depth(&deco_gases, depth, surface_pressure, SALT_WATER_M_PER_BAR);
    diver.step(depth, 3.0, gas.fraction_n2, gas.fraction_he);
    depth -= 3.0;
}
```

Run it and the search reproduces both switch depths from the manual example, 21 m and 6 m, choosing air for everything deeper since that's the only gas in this set rated for it. Those are the depths where each richer gas first drops under 1.6 bar. EAN50 hits 1.6 at 22 m, so 21 m is the first stop it's legal on. Oxygen hits 1.6 at exactly 6 m, which is why every technical diving agency in the world says "oxygen at six metres" as if it were a law of nature.

That "exactly" is doing more work than it looks. Oxygen lands on 6.000 metres only if you use both conventions at once: the flat 1 bar surface from the Nitrox chapter *and* the flat 10 metres per bar from Salt vs. Fresh Water. Put the real numbers in, 1.01325 bar and the 9.9 metres per bar seawater actually manages, and the limit is 5.81 metres. That rounds down to the 3 metre stop, which moves the whole oxygen phase of the dive nearly three metres shallower and lengthens it considerably. The famous number survives on two roundings leaning against each other.

You can watch other people arrive at the same problem from the other direction. Subsurface[^32], the open-source dive planner, computes depth from real salinity and then rounds gas switch depths with a deliberate thumb on the scale. The comment in its source is refreshingly candid:

> Rounding should be towards lower=safer depths but we give a bit of fudge to all to switch to o2 at 6m. So from .9 we round up.

Two implementations, two different fudges, both worked backwards from an answer the diving world had already settled on. EAN50 is robust either way, 22.0 metres by convention and 21.6 in reality, both rounding to the same 21 metre stop. Oxygen is the fragile one, and it's the gas where being wrong costs the most decompression.

The plan the software hands the diver is the earliest, richest gas switch the limit allows at every depth, because that's what minimizes time spent decompressing. The diver's job is to execute it: carry the cylinders the software assumed, switch where it says, and let the computer on their wrist track the consequences one `step` at a time.

## Gradient Factors

Bühlmann's raw M-values tolerate tissue tension right up to the theoretical edge of bubble formation, with nothing held in reserve. His own answer to that was ZH-L16B and ZH-L16C, hand-tuned coefficients for specific compartments. Gradient Factors[^15] are a different answer to the same problem: leave Bühlmann's numbers alone, shrink how much of the M-value the algorithm is allowed to use, and let the diver dial that percentage themselves.

A Gradient Factor is a fraction between 0 and 1, expressed as a pair, GF Low and GF High, like 30/70 or 20/85. At 100/100 it changes nothing and the diver gets the raw model from every earlier chapter. Below that, GF Low controls how conservative the deepest stop is and GF High controls how conservative the final approach to the surface is, with every stop in between sliding linearly from one to the other. A lower GF Low pushes the first stop deeper, spreading the ascent's conservatism across more of the water column instead of saving it all for the shallow end. A lower GF High keeps more margin at the very end of the dive.

Erik Baker introduced the idea in a pair of papers in the late 1990s[^29][^30], and the motivation was less about safety margins in the abstract than about a rivalry. Bubble models, VPM[^13] and RGBM[^14], were gaining ground among technical divers at the time. They don't track dissolved gas tension the way Haldane and Bühlmann do, they simulate the mechanics of bubble nuclei forming and growing in tissue, and that foundation calls for noticeably deeper first stops than a pure ZH-L16 schedule. Divers switching to bubble-model computers reported feeling less fatigued and getting fewer of the minor, sub-clinical symptoms divers call niggles.

Baker's insight was that a full bubble-mechanics simulation wasn't necessary to get the deeper-stop behaviour. Capping how much of the M-value a dissolved-gas model may use, and biasing that cap tighter at depth, produces a schedule that looks a lot like a bubble model's, using math that's dramatically simpler and still grounded in Bühlmann's experimentally fitted numbers rather than a bubble model's less validated assumptions about nucleation and growth.

Whether any of it works better is another question, and the evidence has not been kind to the deep-stop half of the idea. In 2011 the US Navy Experimental Diving Unit published a controlled trial comparing two decompression schedules of identical total length, one with stop time shifted deeper and one with it left shallow. The deep-stop schedule produced more decompression sickness, not less[^31]. The study has been criticised on experimental design, and it tested a specific pair of Navy air profiles rather than gradient factors as such, but it moved the consensus: recommended GF Low values have drifted upward since, and the very deep first stops that were fashionable in 2005 are out of favour. What Gradient Factors reliably give a diver is two transparent, tunable numbers sitting on top of a model they can inspect, which is more than either an opaque bubble simulation or Bühlmann's hand-tuned B and C tables offer.

Mechanically, a Gradient Factor redefines what "safe" means for a compartment. Instead of tolerating tension up to the full M-value, it tolerates the ambient pressure plus a GF-sized fraction of the gap between ambient pressure and the M-value:

$$
M_{gf}(P) = P + GF(P) \times \bigl(M(P) - P\bigr)
$$

At $GF = 1$ this is $M(P)$ again, the raw ceiling from the M Values chapter. At $GF = 0$ it collapses to $P$ itself, no overpressure tolerated at all, equivalent to demanding a compartment be fully equilibrated with ambient pressure before going any shallower. Everything in between is a dial between those two extremes, and $GF(P)$ itself isn't fixed. It slides linearly from `low` at the first stop's pressure to `high` at the surface.

### Implementation

```rust
#[derive(Debug, Clone, Copy)]
struct GradientFactors {
    low: f64,
    high: f64,
}
```

`Diver` needs to remember two new things: which `GradientFactors` it's using, and the pressure of the first stop, since every other stop's gradient factor is defined relative to it:

```rust
struct Diver {
    compartments: [Compartment; 16],
    meters_per_bar: f64,
    surface_pressure: f64,
    gradient_factors: GradientFactors,
    first_stop_pressure: f64,
}
```

The first stop is established once, at the deepest point of the ascent, by asking where the ceiling would be if `GF Low` applied uniformly everywhere. With a single fixed gradient factor, $M_{gf}(P)$ is linear in $P$, since $M(P)$ itself is linear, so this case has a closed-form solution. Solve $M_{gf}(P) = \text{tension}$ for $P$ directly:

```rust
impl Diver {
    fn establish_first_stop(&mut self) {
        let gf_low = self.gradient_factors.low;
        let first_stop = self
            .compartments
            .iter()
            .map(|c| (c.tension() - gf_low * c.a()) / (1.0 - gf_low + gf_low / c.b()))
            .fold(self.surface_pressure, f64::max);
        self.first_stop_pressure = first_stop;
    }
}
```

Once `first_stop_pressure` is known, the sliding gradient factor at any pressure between it and the surface is a straight linear interpolation:

```rust
impl Diver {
    fn gradient_factor_at(&self, pressure: f64) -> f64 {
        let GradientFactors { low, high } = self.gradient_factors;
        if self.first_stop_pressure <= self.surface_pressure {
            return high;
        }
        let fraction = (self.first_stop_pressure - pressure)
            / (self.first_stop_pressure - self.surface_pressure);
        low + (high - low) * fraction.clamp(0.0, 1.0)
    }
}
```

Finding the actual ceiling is where it gets harder. Away from the first stop, $GF(P)$ is no longer constant, it's linear in $P$ too, and multiplying two linear functions of $P$ together makes $M_{gf}(P)$ quadratic. Solving that exactly means reaching for the quadratic formula, which real implementations do. A bisection search is simpler to read, just as correct, and costs a few dozen iterations of arithmetic that a real computer won't notice:

```rust
impl Diver {
    fn allowed_tension(&self, compartment: &Compartment, pressure: f64) -> f64 {
        let gf = self.gradient_factor_at(pressure);
        pressure + gf * (compartment.m_value(pressure) - pressure)
    }

    fn gf_ceiling_pressure_for(&self, compartment: &Compartment) -> f64 {
        let (mut lo, mut hi) = (0.0_f64, compartment.tension());
        for _ in 0..40 {
            let mid = (lo + hi) / 2.0;
            if self.allowed_tension(compartment, mid) < compartment.tension() {
                lo = mid;
            } else {
                hi = mid;
            }
        }
        hi
    }

    fn ceiling_pressure(&self) -> f64 {
        self.compartments
            .iter()
            .map(|c| self.gf_ceiling_pressure_for(c))
            .fold(self.surface_pressure, f64::max)
    }

    fn ceiling_depth(&self) -> f64 {
        ((self.ceiling_pressure() - self.surface_pressure) * self.meters_per_bar).max(0.0)
    }
}
```

`gf_ceiling_pressure_for` searches for the pressure where a compartment's tension sits on `allowed_tension`. `hi` starts at the compartment's own tension because a compartment can never need more pressure than its current tension to be safe, and the search narrows from there. Run the 40 metre, 25 minute air dive through this at 100/100 and the ceiling comes out at 6.5 metres, identical to the plain M-value ceiling, as it should. Switch to 30/70 and it jumps to 17.9 metres, nearly three times deeper.

### From Ceiling to Schedule

Everything so far answers the first half of the question this article opened with. A ceiling says how shallow the diver can be right now. It says nothing about how long they have to wait before going shallower, which is the half a diver actually reads off their wrist.

Getting from one to the other is a loop. Round the ceiling up to the next stop increment, ascend to it, then sit there calling `step` until the ceiling lifts past that increment, and repeat. Stops are conventionally spaced 3 metres apart.

The ascent between stops is where Schreiner's equation finally earns its place. A diver moving from 40 metres to 18 metres at 10 metres per minute spends 2.2 minutes at continuously falling pressure, and `step` has no way to express that. So `transit` gets written out properly, with the rate term the Ascent Rates chapter introduced:

```rust
const LN_2: f64 = std::f64::consts::LN_2;

fn schreiner(current: f64, p_alv: f64, rate: f64, half_time: f64, t: f64) -> f64 {
    let k = LN_2 / half_time;
    p_alv + rate * (t - 1.0 / k) - (p_alv - current - rate / k) * (-k * t).exp()
}

impl Compartment {
    fn transit(&mut self, p_n2: f64, p_he: f64, rate_n2: f64, rate_he: f64, t: f64) {
        self.n2_tension = schreiner(self.n2_tension, p_n2, rate_n2, self.n2_half_time, t);
        self.he_tension = schreiner(self.he_tension, p_he, rate_he, self.he_half_time, t);
    }
}

impl Diver {
    fn transit(&mut self, from_m: f64, to_m: f64, elapsed_min: f64, fraction_n2: f64, fraction_he: f64) {
        let from = self.surface_pressure + from_m / self.meters_per_bar - WATER_VAPOR_PRESSURE;
        let to = self.surface_pressure + to_m / self.meters_per_bar - WATER_VAPOR_PRESSURE;
        let rate_n2 = fraction_n2 * (to - from) / elapsed_min;
        let rate_he = fraction_he * (to - from) / elapsed_min;
        for c in &mut self.compartments {
            c.transit(fraction_n2 * from, fraction_he * from, rate_n2, rate_he, elapsed_min);
        }
    }
}
```

With that in hand the schedule is the loop described above, using `transit` to move and `step` to wait:

```rust
const STOP_INTERVAL: f64 = 3.0;      // metres between stops
const ASCENT_RATE_M_PER_MIN: f64 = 10.0;

#[derive(Debug, Clone, Copy)]
struct Stop {
    depth_m: f64,
    minutes: u32,
}

fn next_stop_depth(ceiling_m: f64) -> f64 {
    (ceiling_m / STOP_INTERVAL).ceil() * STOP_INTERVAL
}

impl Diver {
    fn ascent_schedule(&mut self, from_depth_m: f64, n2: f64, he: f64) -> Vec<Stop> {
        self.establish_first_stop();
        let mut schedule = Vec::new();
        let mut depth = from_depth_m;

        loop {
            let target = next_stop_depth(self.ceiling_depth());
            if target >= depth {
                break;
            }

            let travel = (depth - target) / ASCENT_RATE_M_PER_MIN;
            self.transit(depth, target, travel, n2, he);
            depth = target;
            if depth <= 0.0 {
                break;
            }

            let mut minutes = 0;
            while next_stop_depth(self.ceiling_depth()) >= depth && minutes < 600 {
                self.step(depth, 1.0, n2, he);
                minutes += 1;
            }
            if minutes > 0 {
                schedule.push(Stop { depth_m: depth, minutes });
            }
        }

        schedule
    }
}
```

Run the 40 metre, 25 minute air dive through it at three different settings and the cost of conservatism stops being abstract:

```
GF 100/100   6m/5min, 3m/9min                                            14 min of stops
GF  40/85   15m/1min, 12m/3min, 9m/3min, 6m/6min, 3m/11min              24 min of stops
GF  30/70   18m/1min, 15m/2min, 12m/4min, 9m/5min, 6m/10min, 3m/18min   40 min of stops
```

Tightening from 100/100 to 30/70 nearly triples the time spent hanging on a line, and most of that extra time lands at the 3 metre stop rather than the deep end. The 30/70 schedule starts at 18 metres, which is the 17.9 metre first stop from the previous section rounded up to the next increment, exactly as it should.

That last sentence is a correction. The first version of this chapter used `step` for the travel legs instead of `transit`, charging the whole ascent at the shallower of the two depths, and it produced a schedule that started at 15 metres rather than 18. I wrote a confident paragraph explaining why: the fast compartments off-gas during the 2.2 minute ascent, the ceiling lifts past 18 metres before the diver gets there, and the stop evaporates on the way up. It reads well and it is completely wrong. Charging a leg at the shallower depth understates the pressure the diver was actually under for most of it, which under-loads every compartment and produces exactly that spurious lift. The stop was never evaporating. My integration was leaking. Swapping in Schreiner's equation put back roughly four minutes of decompression across every setting and the 18 metre stop with it.

I'm leaving the wrong version described rather than quietly deleting it, because the failure mode is the interesting part. The bug produced a plausible physical story, and a plausible physical story is much harder to doubt than an obviously broken number. Nothing crashed, nothing came out negative, no assertion fired. The schedule just came out a little short, in the direction that makes a diver surface sooner.

The 100/100 schedule still has 14 minutes of stops for a dive whose ceiling was only 6.5 metres. A ceiling of 6.5 metres doesn't mean "surface after a short pause", it means the diver may not go shallower than 6.5 metres until enough nitrogen has left, and clearing the last few metres is the slow part. That's the shape of every decompression schedule: brief stops deep, and a long one just under the surface.

## Rebreathers

Every gas fraction fed into `step` so far has come from a cylinder valve, fixed the moment the diver picked what to breathe. Open circuit works the way the name implies: inhale from a tank, exhale into the water. The fraction of nitrogen or helium in a given breath is whatever was in the tank, unchanged, for the whole time that gas is in use, and every exhaled breath, still carrying most of its original oxygen, is gone for good. A rebreather changes that at the root. Exhaled gas is kept, cleaned, and reused, and the gas a diver breathes is recomputed by the device, breath by breath, for the entire dive.

A closed-circuit rebreather, or CCR, is built around a sealed loop. A breath leaves the diver's lungs into a flexible bag called a counterlung, passes through a canister of chemical absorbent, usually soda lime, that scrubs the CO2 out, and comes back around ready to be inhaled again. Nothing in that loop replaces the oxygen the diver consumed, so two cylinders feed into it: a small one of pure oxygen, topping up what metabolism used, and a diluent, some breathable inert mix, usually air or a trimix, added to keep the loop's volume roughly constant as ambient pressure rises and falls. Because the loop only replaces what gets consumed, rather than dumping and refilling an entire lungful with every breath, a CCR diver can stay underwater for hours on cylinders barely bigger than the ones open-circuit divers empty in a fraction of the time.

### Set Point

What gets injected into the loop, and when, is decided by a control loop of its own. Oxygen sensors read the loop's partial pressure of oxygen continuously, and whenever it drifts below a target the diver sets, called the setpoint, a solenoid fires and injects pure oxygen until the loop is back at target.

Holding PO2 constant is what makes the gas math different from everything built so far. On open circuit, the oxygen fraction is fixed and PO2 climbs with depth, $PO_2 = \text{fraction}_{O_2} \times P_{amb}$, which is the basis of the MOD calculation from the Nitrox chapter. On a rebreather, $PO_2$ is pinned to the setpoint, so the oxygen fraction has to shrink as ambient pressure rises to keep the product constant:

$$
\text{fraction}_{O_2}(P_{amb}) = \frac{\text{setpoint}}{P_{amb}}
$$

Whatever fraction isn't oxygen is inert gas. Since only oxygen is being added or removed, that inert fraction keeps the same internal ratio of nitrogen to helium as the diluent, it grows to fill however much room the shrinking oxygen fraction leaves behind:

```rust
#[derive(Debug, Clone, Copy)]
struct Diluent {
    fraction_n2: f64,
    fraction_he: f64,
}

impl Diluent {
    fn fraction_o2(&self) -> f64 {
        1.0 - self.fraction_n2 - self.fraction_he
    }
}

struct Rebreather {
    setpoint: f64,
    diluent: Diluent,
}

impl Rebreather {
    fn loop_gas(&self, ambient_pressure: f64) -> (f64, f64) {
        let fraction_o2 = (self.setpoint / ambient_pressure)
            .min(1.0)
            .max(self.diluent.fraction_o2());
        let fraction_inert = 1.0 - fraction_o2;
        let diluent_inert = self.diluent.fraction_n2 + self.diluent.fraction_he;
        if diluent_inert <= 0.0 {
            return (0.0, 0.0);
        }
        let fraction_n2 = fraction_inert * (self.diluent.fraction_n2 / diluent_inert);
        let fraction_he = fraction_inert * (self.diluent.fraction_he / diluent_inert);
        (fraction_n2, fraction_he)
    }
}
```

The two clamps on `fraction_o2` cover the cases where the setpoint isn't reachable. Near the surface, `setpoint / ambient_pressure` can exceed 1.0, asking for more oxygen than physically exists, so it's capped at pure O2, which is what a CCR loop looks like sitting at the surface. At depth the opposite problem shows up. `.max(self.diluent.fraction_o2())` stops the fraction from dropping below what the diluent supplies on its own, because there's no way to have less oxygen in the loop than the diluent already contains without diluting further, and the rebreather isn't carrying anything to dilute with.

Run a 21/35 trimix diluent through `loop_gas` at a 1.2 bar setpoint and the shape of the problem shows up immediately. At 15 metres the loop holds 48% oxygen. By 45 metres that's down to 22%, with nitrogen and helium filling the rest in the same 44:35 ratio the diluent started with. Compare that to breathing the same 21/35 trimix on open circuit, fixed at 21% oxygen throughout, and the two diverge sharply: at 15 metres the rebreather is delivering more than twice the oxygen fraction, and correspondingly less inert gas to load into every compartment. That's the appeal for technical divers. PO2 held near the top of a safe range for the whole dive, not just at the surface, means slower nitrogen and helium loading throughout the bottom phase.

It also means the diluent has to be chosen for the deepest point of the dive, not just for narcosis and gas density the way open-circuit bottom mix is. Push that same 21/35 diluent to 60 metres and the diluent's own 21% oxygen produces a PO2 of 1.47 bar, past the 1.4 bar working limit from the Nitrox chapter. The deep clamp in `loop_gas` kicks in and the loop can no longer hold the 1.2 setpoint at all. A CCR diver planning that depth needs a leaner diluent. It's the same MOD arithmetic from two chapters ago, applied to a diluent instead of a single fixed mix.

The other half of the appeal shows up on ascent. Nothing stops a diver from raising the setpoint once they're shallow enough for the higher PO2 to be safe. The same electronics that held it low at depth to manage oxygen toxicity risk can push it up during decompression stops to accelerate off-gassing, the effect the Multigas chapter got from physically switching to a richer deco cylinder. On open circuit that meant carrying an extra tank and executing a gas switch by hand. On a rebreather it's a number typed into a controller. `loop_gas` recomputes the fractions for the new setpoint and they feed into `Diver::step` the way every other gas source in this article has. The decompression math underneath has no idea whether those fractions came from a cylinder valve or a solenoid, and no need to know.

### Multiple Oxygen Sensors

Everything in the previous section assumed the controller knows the loop's PO2, but that number comes from a physical sensor, and one sensor is one point of failure. Galvanic oxygen sensors are small electrochemical cells that consume themselves slowly as they work. Near the end of their life they go current-limited: the output stops rising with PO2 and flattens out, so the cell reads plausibly at 0.7 bar and badly low at 1.3. That's the failure a diver most needs to catch, because a sensor under-reading PO2 tells the controller to inject more oxygen than the loop needs, and the diver ends up hyperoxic while the display insists everything is fine. Cells can also fail abruptly, flooded by a leak or by caustic scrubber breakthrough. Because of all this, CCR controllers don't trust one sensor. They read three, and vote.

A simple, common approach: take the median of the three readings, treat any sensor within some tolerance of that median as agreeing, and only trust the result if at least two of the three agree with each other.

```rust
const SENSOR_AGREEMENT_TOLERANCE: f64 = 0.10; // bar

fn consensus_po2(readings: [f64; 3]) -> Option<f64> {
    let mut sorted = readings;
    sorted.sort_by(|a, b| a.partial_cmp(b).unwrap());
    let median = sorted[1];

    let agreeing: Vec<f64> = readings
        .into_iter()
        .filter(|r| (r - median).abs() <= SENSOR_AGREEMENT_TOLERANCE)
        .collect();

    if agreeing.len() >= 2 {
        Some(agreeing.iter().sum::<f64>() / agreeing.len() as f64)
    } else {
        None
    }
}
```

Three sensors reading 1.19, 1.20, and 1.21 bar all agree, and `consensus_po2` averages them to 1.20. Let one fail low, to 0.40 bar while the other two still read 1.20 and 1.21, and the median stays with the healthy pair. The failed sensor sits 0.8 bar away from it, well outside tolerance, and drops out automatically, leaving 1.205 from the two that agree. If all three land far enough apart, say 0.50, 1.20, and 1.80 bar, there's no way to tell which if any is correct, and `consensus_po2` returns `None` rather than guessing. A real controller treats that `None` as an emergency. PO2 is now unknowable, so it can't safely keep running the loop. It alarms and tells the diver to bail out to an independent, known-good gas supply.

Voting has a failure mode of its own, and it's the reason CCR divers are taught to distrust their electronics. Two cells that fail the same way at the same time outvote the one healthy cell, and this is not hypothetical: cells bought together, installed together, and aged together in the same warm humid loop tend to reach end of life together. The counter is procedural rather than algorithmic. Divers stagger cell replacement dates, and they periodically flush the loop with pure oxygen at a known depth to check every cell against a PO2 they can compute by hand.

## Conclusion

This article started with a single question: given everything a diver has breathed so far, how shallow can they safely be right now, and how long until they can go shallower.

The Simplified Algorithm chapter built the smallest thing that could answer it: 16 compartments tracking nitrogen with Haldane's equation, 16 M-values bounding how much each compartment may hold, and a four-step loop to update, evaluate, find the worst case, and turn it into a depth. Nothing after that chapter touched the loop. Six subchapters of Bühlmann ZH-L16 taught `step` to accept a different water density, a different starting altitude, a different oxygen fraction, a second inert gas, and gas switches mid-dive, and none of them changed what the loop did once it had its numbers. Gradient Factors reshaped the ceiling, tighter near the first stop and looser near the surface, but the shape doing the reshaping was still $M(P)$ scaled by a fraction, and turning that ceiling into an actual stop schedule took nothing more than moving with `transit`, waiting with `step`, and repeating until the ceiling reached the surface. Rebreathers looked like they should break everything, since the breathing gas changes with every metre of depth, and turned out to be one more way of computing `fraction_n2` and `fraction_he` before handing them to the same `step`.

Then there's the boundary of what any of this proves. This implementation checks out against itself, against Bühlmann's published constants, and against the primary references, and it's still 16 theoretical compartments standing in for a body nobody has been able to measure directly. A bisection search stood in for a quadratic solve. Descents are still charged with the constant-pressure form, so the model is slightly optimistic about the way down even though the way up is integrated properly. VPM and RGBM got discussed and never implemented. Cold, exertion, dehydration, patent foramen ovale, and the dozen other individual factors that make two divers on the same profile end up with different outcomes never entered the model at all[^19]. The flying-after-diving chapter showed what happens when you take the model's word for something it was never fitted to predict. None of this changes when a diver should trust this code: never, for planning an actual dive. That's what the warning at the top of the article was for, and it's just as true down here.

What I hope comes across, more than any single formula, is the shape of the problem. A dive computer isn't magic, and it isn't really one algorithm either. It's a small simulation, a handful of exponentials and a search for the worst case among them, running continuously against whatever numbers a diver, or a rebreather's electronics, feed it. None of that crossed my mind at 16, in the Red Sea. I just wanted to see the fish. It's been worth coming back, years later, to find the algorithm that had been quietly keeping me safe the whole time.
