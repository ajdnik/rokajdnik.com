## The Decompression Problem

Before we delve into how the algorithm works, we need a rough grasp of decompression theory. Divers breathe gas to stay alive, usually air, the same as we breathe on the surface[^atmosphere]. Physics and anatomy force them to breathe it at higher pressure than we do on land, and the deeper they go, the higher that pressure climbs.

Pressure in this article is measured in bar[^bar]. One bar is roughly the weight of the atmosphere pressing down on you at sea level. Descend 10 metres into the ocean and the water above you adds another bar, so a diver at 30 metres is breathing gas at about 4 bar, four times the pressure at the surface.

Right now, sitting on the surface, your body is already full of nitrogen. Air is about 78% nitrogen, and Henry's Law[^henryslaw] says the amount of a gas that dissolves into a liquid is proportional to the partial pressure[^partialpressure] of that gas. At 1 bar of air, your blood and tissues sit at equilibrium with roughly 0.76 bar of dissolved nitrogen, provided you have been breathing that air long enough to equilibrate.

Descending changes the pressure, and therefore the equilibrium. A diver at 30 metres inhales nitrogen at about 3.1 bar instead of 0.76, so it diffuses out of the lungs, into the blood, and into the tissues until they catch up. This is on-gassing, and on its own it still isn't a problem. We'll pretend nitrogen is “inert”, meaning the body doesn't metabolise it, and we'll conveniently ignore nitrogen narcosis[^narcosis].

The trouble starts on the way back up. Think of a carbonated drink, bottled under high pressure so large amounts of CO₂ dissolve into the liquid. Pop the cap, the pressure drops, the equilibrium breaks, and the gas escapes as bubbles. The same happens in a diver's body during ascent. Similarly in our body nitrogen normally leaves the tissues dissolved in blood and diffuses into the lungs to be exhaled. Bubbles can also form, sometimes without symptoms, but they can cause decompression sickness.[^dcs].

Now shake that bottle and open it slowly. Crack the cap, let some pressure out, wait, crack it further. The drink stays in the bottle. That's the whole idea behind a controlled ascent, and what decompression algorithms are built to schedule.

> **A decompression algorithm answers one question:**
>
> Given everything I have breathed so far, how shallow can I safely be right now and how long must I wait before I can go shallower?

## Simplified Algorithm 

We'll implement arguably the most popular decompression algorithm, the Bühlmann ZH-L16[^buhlmann] with Gradient Factors. But we'll do it in piecemeal fashion, starting with a simpler version, not the fully featured one present in dive software. The Bühlmann ZH-L16 is commonly used in dive computers and for building dive tables[^dtables] meant for recreational[^recreational] and technical[^technical] SCUBA divers, while commercial[^commercial] and military divers normally use different algorithms[^thalmann][^dciem] custom designed for their needs. Besides ZH-L16, recreational and technical dive computers also use VPM-B[^vpm] or RGBM[^rgbm] models, but the focus here will be ZH-L16 as it seems to be most widely used.

We'll start with a simplified version of the algorithm to master the basic concepts. For starters we'll focus on building an algorithm that can model a single dive to arbitrary depth, the dive will start at sea level and will be an ocean dive, meaning the diver will be diving in saltwater. The diver will also be breathing a single gas mixture of air. Parameters like repetitive dives, altitude of the dive, multiple breathing mixtures, water type all affect the physics of decompression and make the decompression model more complex. We'll also skip gradient factors[^gradientfactors], for now.

### Modeling the Human Body

A dive computer does not measure dissolved nitrogen in the diver's tissues. Instead it measures pressure and elapsed time, combines them with the selected breathing gas, and mathematically models what's happening in your body based on the input parameters. These models are generic, they aren't customized for each person and their body composition. They were built through decades of experiments, starting with John Scott Haldane[^haldane], alongside Arthur Boycott and Guybon Damant[^boycott], who in 1908 proposed the first such model.

They treated the body as a set of theoretical compartments, five in total, each a sponge soaking up and releasing gas at its own fixed rate. Bühlmann ZH-L16[^buhlmann] uses sixteen, numbered fastest to slowest. Compartment 1's half-time is 4 minutes and stands in for well-perfused, blood-rich tissue such as blood itself or the brain; compartment 16's is 635 minutes, over 10 hours, standing in for poorly perfused tissue like fat, ligaments, or cartilage. None correspond to a specific organ. They're a mathematical convenience covering the range of speeds real tissue might load and unload nitrogen at, and the 16 in ZH-L16 refers to them.

A half-time is how long a compartment takes to close half the gap between its current nitrogen tension and where it's heading. Drop a diver to a new depth and every compartment starts chasing the new inspired pressure, none of them jumping straight to it: after one half-time each has closed 50% of the gap, after two 75%, after three 87.5%, the same halving pattern as radioactive decay. A compartment never fully saturates in the mathematics, but after about six half-times it's close enough to treat as done. Compartment 1 is effectively saturated within 24 minutes; compartment 16 needs over 60 hours.

Each compartment updates independently using the same exponential formula, known as Haldane's equation:

$$
P(t) = P_{alv} + (P_0 - P_{alv}) \times 2^{-t / \text{half-time}}
$$

$P(t)$ is the compartment's nitrogen tension after time $t$, $P_0$ is what it started at, and $P_{alv}$ is the alveolar pressure of nitrogen the diver is currently breathing, essentially the partial pressure of nitrogen at the current depth. $\text{half-time}$ is the compartment's defining property. Because every compartment runs this formula on its own clock, a single dive profile produces 16 different loading curves, and the algorithm tracks all of them at once.

Let's start turning this into code. A compartment only needs to remember two numbers, its half-time and its current tension, and it only needs to know how to do one thing, update itself given the pressure the diver is currently breathing and how long they've been breathing it:

```rust
#[derive(Debug, Clone, Copy)]
struct Compartment {
    half_time: f64, // minutes
    tension: f64,   // current N2 tension, in bar
}

impl Compartment {
    fn constant_depth(&mut self, p_alv: f64, duration: f64) {
        let decay = 2f64.powf(-duration / self.half_time);
        self.tension = p_alv + (self.tension - p_alv) * decay;
    }
}
```

The code assumes, similarly as Haldane's equation, that the inspired pressure didn't change throught the interval. Real dive computers normally sample pressure multiple times per second, filtering readings to reduce noise, then they update compartment tensions once per second to mitigate Haldane's assumption and to provide a more accurate modeling of tissues.

We'll model 16 tissue compartments like Bühlmann's, and use his published half-times in ZH-L16[^buhlmann].

```rust
const COMPARTMENTS: usize = 16;

// in minutes
const HALF_TIMES: [f64; COMPARTMENTS] = [
    4.0, 8.0, 12.5, 18.5, 27.0, 38.3, 54.3, 77.0, 109.0, 146.0, 187.0, 239.0, 305.0, 390.0,
    498.0, 635.0,
];
```

A diver who's been breathing surface air for long enough starts every dive with all 16 compartments already at equilibrium, so we can build the whole tissue model from that single starting tension:

```rust
fn equilibrated(surface_n2: f64) -> [Compartment; COMPARTMENTS] {
    HALF_TIMES.map(|half_time| Compartment {
        half_time,
        tension: surface_n2,
    })
}
```

### M Values

Tracking nitrogen loading is only half the problem. A tissue tension on its own isn't useful, to answer the question posed at the beginning of the article, we need to compare the tissue tension against another value. M-value[^mvalues] is a ceiling value for a compartment: the tissue's maximum tolerated nitrogen tension at a given ambient pressure. Exceeding M-value doesn't mean a diver will automatically develop decompression sickness, staying below the M-value also doesn't mean they won't. The value is just a statistical limit where keeping tissues below their M-value tension significantly reduces the risk of decompression sickness.

Robert Workman introduced them at the US Navy in 1965[^workman], replacing Haldane's fixed 2:1 ratio with the observation that each compartment tolerates a different absolute overpressure, and that the tolerance changes with depth. Bühlmann used Workman's M-value equation, but he modified it to use ambient pressure instead of depth, which made it portable to altitude diving[^chapman]:

$$
M = a + \frac{P_{amb}}{b}
$$

$P_{amb}$ is the ambient pressure at the diver's current depth, and $M$ is the maximum nitrogen tension that compartment is allowed to hold at that pressure. Each of the 16 compartments has its own $a$ and $b$. Fast compartments have M-value lines that tolerate a large overpressure, while slow compartments have lower tolerated gradients.

Comparing tissue tensions against their M-values gives us the ceiling depth, the shallowest depth at which no compartment would exceed its M-value. As soon as one compartment's tension would cross its line, that compartment becomes the controlling compartment, and the diver has to stop ascending until it off-gasses enough for the ceiling to lift.

Back in the code, each `Compartment` picks up its `a` and `b` coefficients, plus a way to evaluate its own M-value at a given ambient pressure and, by rearranging that same line, the ambient pressure at which its current tension would sit exactly on the M-value:

```rust
#[derive(Debug, Clone, Copy)]
struct Compartment {
    half_time: f64, // minutes
    tension: f64,   // current N2 tension, in bar
    a: f64,         // M-value coefficient
    b: f64,         // M-value coefficient
}

impl Compartment {
    fn constant_depth(&mut self, p_alv: f64, duration: f64) {
        let decay = 2f64.powf(-duration / self.half_time);
        self.tension = p_alv + (self.tension - p_alv) * decay;
    }

    fn m_value(&self, ambient: f64) -> f64 {
        self.a + ambient / self.b
    }

    fn ceiling_pressure(&self) -> f64 {
        (self.tension - self.a) * self.b
    }
}
```

`ceiling_pressure` is the M-value formula solved for `ambient` instead of `M`: at what ambient pressure would this compartment's tension sit exactly on its M-value? That's the compartment's own ceiling. Bühlmann published `a` and `b` alongside the half-times, so we can copy his homework:

```rust
const COMPARTMENTS: usize = 16;

struct GasTable {
    half_times: [f64; COMPARTMENTS],
    a: [f64; COMPARTMENTS],
    b: [f64; COMPARTMENTS],
}

static ZH_L16C_N2: GasTable = GasTable {
    half_times: [
        5.0, 8.0, 12.5, 18.5, 27.0, 38.3, 54.3, 77.0, 109.0, 146.0, 187.0, 239.0, 305.0, 390.0,
        498.0, 635.0,
    ],
    a: [
        1.1696, 1.0000, 0.8618, 0.7562, 0.6200, 0.5043, 0.4410, 0.4000, 0.3750, 0.3500, 0.3295,
        0.3065, 0.2835, 0.2610, 0.2480, 0.2327,
    ],
    b: [
        0.5578, 0.6514, 0.7222, 0.7825, 0.8126, 0.8434, 0.8693, 0.8910, 0.9092, 0.9222, 0.9319,
        0.9403, 0.9477, 0.9544, 0.9602, 0.9653,
    ],
};
```

The `HALF_TIMES` array from the previous section has been absorbed into `ZH_L16C_N2.half_times`. We've expanded the model to include `a` and `b` arrays from the Subsurface's published C implementation[^subsurface]. The $a$ and $b$ coefficients are for nitrogen specifically, other gases have different coefficients. Bühlmann produced 3 sets of a/b coefficients for nitrogen. A, B, and C, and which one a given piece of software implements changes how conservative the resulting model is.

ZH-L16A[^buhlmann] is the original, purely mathematical dataset, where Bühlmann derived $a$ and $b$ directly from each compartment's half-time. ZH-L16B tightens the $a$ coefficient for some compartments, while leaving the rest of the table equal to A[^buhlmann]. Bühlmann recommended B for table calculation and C for dive computers.[^mvalues] Rounding a table toward greater depth and longer time can add conservatism; rounding in the opposite direction can remove it.

### Putting It All Together

With Haldane's equation to track nitrogen loading and M-values to define a model ceiling, the algorithm itself is mostly a loop. At every step of a dive, sampled every second, it does the same three things:

1. Read absolute ambient pressure from the pressure sensor, or convert a planned depth into pressure, then calculate inspired nitrogen pressure.
2. Update all 16 compartments using Haldane's equation, based on how long the diver has been at that depth.
3. Find the controlling compartment based on M-value parameters for each compartment and determine the ceiling pressure.

If the ceiling depth is zero, that means the diver can surface safely, if it starts increasing it means the diver needs to wait at the defined depth before surfacing. It's worth mentioning that dive computers usually have a safety stop programmed in, which is normally configured to triger even when ceiling depth is 0. It's there for extra safety and is usually a 3 minute stop at 5 meters. The stop is included because, as previously mentioned, the algorithm can't model diver's body and tissues, it's just a series of statistical curves fitted over a population. This stop isn't a decompression stop, those are defined when ceiling depth is greater than 0.

Expanding the code, we can define a `DecoModel` to house all the logic. `DecoModel::new` builds the tissue model the way `equilibrated` did, now also seeding each compartment's `a` and `b`:

```rust
const SURFACE_PRESSURE: f64 = 1.01325; // bar, the standard atmosphere
const WATER_VAPOUR: f64 = 0.0627; // bar, in the lungs at body temperature
const FRACTION_N2: f64 = 0.79; // breathing air

#[derive(Debug, Clone, Copy)]
struct DecoModel {
    compartments: [Compartment; COMPARTMENTS],
}

impl DecoModel {
    fn new(table: &GasTable) -> Self {
        let surface_n2 = FRACTION_N2 * (SURFACE_PRESSURE - WATER_VAPOUR);
        DecoModel {
            compartments: std::array::from_fn(|i| Compartment {
                half_time: table.half_times[i],
                tension: surface_n2,
                a: table.a[i],
                b: table.b[i],
            }),
        }
    }
}
```

We need to account for `WATER_VAPOUR` because inhaled air is humidified in the lungs before nitrogen's partial pressure is calculated; that fixed 0.0627 bar[^buhlmann] displaces breathing gas and never carries nitrogen. `FRACTION_N2` is 0.79 because while air's true nitrogen content is 78.08%, diving convention lumps argon and the other trace gases in with nitrogen and treats the result as one inert gas. `SURFACE_PRESSURE` is 1.01325, the standard atmosphere. The M-value equations themselves use absolute ambient pressure and can be evaluated at other surface pressures.

Then we expand `DecoModel` with `record` and `ceiling`, which will be used to implement the three-item loop described above:

```rust
impl DecoModel {
    fn record(&mut self, depth_m: f64, duration: f64) {
        let ambient = SURFACE_PRESSURE + depth_m / 10.0;
        let p_alv = FRACTION_N2 * (ambient - WATER_VAPOUR);
        for c in &mut self.compartments {
            c.constant_depth(p_alv, duration);
        }
    }

    fn ceiling(&self) -> f64 {
        let ceiling_pressure = self
            .compartments
            .iter()
            .map(Compartment::ceiling_pressure)
            .fold(SURFACE_PRESSURE, f64::max);
        ((ceiling_pressure - SURFACE_PRESSURE) * 10.0).max(0.0)
    }
}
```
We'll run `record` to log each discrete time step and update tissue tension for each of the 16 compartments, and we'll use `ceiling` to find the controlling tissue compartment: taking the highest ceiling pressure across all 16 compartments, since that's the greatest minimum ambient pressure any compartment requires, and convert it to a depth, clamped at zero.

Here's how we can simulate a 45 minute dive at 30 meters, breathing air:

```rust
let mut model = DecoModel::new(&ZH_L16C_N2);

for minute in 1..=45 {
    model.record(30.0, 1.0);
    let ceiling = model.ceiling();
    if ceiling > 0.0 {
        println!("{minute} min: ceiling now at {ceiling:.1}m, ascent must stop there");
    }
}
```

This simulation simplifies a few things, for one it assumes the diver just teleports themselves down to 30 meters and then teleport themselves to the ceiling depth, it doesn't account for descent and ascent. The other simplification is that the diver will remain at 30 meters throughout the whole dive, while is also inaccurate. Real dive profiles[^dprofile] are much more noisy. Running the code above we'd see no ceiling for the first 16 minutes. At 17 minutes a ceiling appears, 14 centimetres of it, and it grows the longer the bottom time stretches: 0.95 m at 20 minutes, 2.9 m at 30, 4.9 m at 45.

We can use a mathematical shortcut to compute no-stop time[^ndl] for dives like these. Modern computers usually have a "Plan Dive" feature which displays no-stop time for a selected depth so the diver knows how long they could stay at a given depth without hitting the ceiling and having to perform decompression stops. We just need to solve Haldane's equation for when the tissue tension crosses the surface M-value $M_s$, and then use the below equation to get the time: 

$$
t = -h\log_2\left(\frac{P_i-M_s}{P_i-P_0}\right)
$$

Finding the compartment where this time $t$ is the smallest gives us the no-stop time. If no compartment crosses their surface M-value $M_s$ at a given depth, then the diver can theoretically stay at that depth indefinitely and always be able to surface without any ceilings. Similarly as dive computer's "Plan Dive" function this calculation also assumes constant depth and no gas changes during ascent.

```rust
#[derive(Debug, Clone, Copy, PartialEq)]
enum NoStopTime {
    AlreadyExceeded,
    Finite(f64),
    Unlimited,
}

impl DecoModel {
    fn no_stop_time(&self, depth_m: f64) -> NoStopTime {
        let inspired = FRACTION_N2 * (SURFACE_PRESSURE + depth_m / 10.0 - WATER_VAPOUR);
        let mut earliest = f64::INFINITY;
        for c in &self.compartments {
            let limit = c.m_value(SURFACE_PRESSURE);
            if c.tension > limit {
                return NoStopTime::AlreadyExceeded;
            }
            if inspired > limit {
                let time = -c.half_time * ((inspired - limit) / (inspired - c.tension)).log2();
                earliest = earliest.min(time);
            }
        }
        if earliest.is_finite() {
            NoStopTime::Finite(earliest)
        } else {
            NoStopTime::Unlimited
        }
    }
}
```

At 30 metres on air from surface-equilibrated tissues, this returns 16.54 minutes. At sufficiently shallow depths it returns `Unlimited`, this infinite ceiling only applies to nitrogen loading in tissues, when doing multi hour dives at shallower depths we also need to account for gas supply limits and thermal exposure limits. Those limits are normaly managed by divers themselves, not dive algorithms.

### Ascent Rates

The model we were using so far relied on frequent updates through `record` calls to approximate changing pressure through discrete constant-pressure intervals. For a dive computer which samples ambient pressure every second and updates the tissue compartments, that's good enough. But if we'd building a dive planning software, we need to have a way to account for pressure change when computing tissue tension. To solve this Schreiner and Kelley[^schreiner] published an equation in 1971, which extends Haldane's equation with a term for a linearly changing inspired pressure:

$$
P(t) = P_{alv_0} + R\left(t - \frac{1}{k}\right) - \left(P_{alv_0} - P_0 - \frac{R}{k}\right)e^{-kt}
$$

$R$ is the rate the inspired inert gas pressure changes at, in bar per minute, and $k$ is $\ln 2$ divided by the half-time. Set $R$ to zero and the whole thing collapses back into Haldane's equation. Ascending slower gives the fast compartments more time to dump gas before the ceiling is evaluated. Ascending faster reduces that time. Ascent-rate policies vary from dive school to dive school as well as the kind of diving one is doing. Most dive software uses ascent rate of about 10 metres per minute and descent rates of 20 meters per minute when computing dive profiles. 

We'll add `changing_depth` to  `Compartment` implementing the equation:

```rust
impl Compartment {
    fn changing_depth(&mut self, p_alv: f64, rate: f64, duration: f64) {
        let k = std::f64::consts::LN_2 / self.half_time;
        self.tension = p_alv + rate * (duration - 1.0 / k)
            - (p_alv - self.tension - rate / k) * (-k * duration).exp();
    }
}
```

Comparing the function to `constant_depth` it takes in one additional parameter `rate`, which is signed, negative while ascending, since inspired pressure falls, positive while descending.

## Where This Leaves Us

The decompression model we've implemented so far is simple enough to write on a napkin, we're modeling 16 tissue compartments, using Haldane's equation to calculate tissue pressure and M-values to determine compartment limits. We've implemented a simple loop which can be used to simulate a dive or update it in real-time. Lastly we've implemented a no-stop time computation and accounted for ascent rates.

That said, the model still makes a lot of assumptions. It assumes seawater, sea level, one dive, and one cylinder of air. Most importantly, the model only tries answering one part of the original question we defined: what's the shallowest depth permitted by the model. But it can't help us figure out how long the diver should stay at a certain depth before ascending.
